Which transaction-monitoring alerts get escalated?

An end-to-end look at 20,000 alerts and 10 million transactions: what the data really contains, which behaviors separate escalated alerts from dismissed ones, and how we turned each alert's history into a probability.

Section 1

Approach

Each alert (signal_id) comes with a many-to-one history of transactions. We aggregate that history into one feature vector per alert, then train models to rank alerts by escalation probability. ROC-AUC is the metric, so the whole pipeline is built around an honest out-of-fold estimate. No fold ever picks its own hyperparameters, tree count, or blend weight.

  1. Audit the dataVerify schemas and joins, check timestamps against alert dates, and look for clipping and artifacts.
  2. ExploreCompare escalated and dismissed alerts on volume, direction, type mix, amounts, recency, and timing.
  3. Engineer features324 per-alert aggregates. Every feature is computed the same way on train and test; the only statistics fit globally (per-type amount normalization) come from train.
  4. Validate honestlyStratified 5-fold CV. Tuning and blend weights use a separate fold split. The pipeline passes a permuted-label test and an adversarial check.
  5. Model & blendA 3-seed LightGBM bag blended in logit space with an L2 logistic regression, then Platt-calibrated.

Direction (kirim_chiqim): 75.7% of transactions are inflows (kirim) and 24.3% are outflows (chiqim). Type (tranzaksiya_turi): card 53.8%, bank transfer 39.4%, cash 6.3%, international 0.46%. miqdor_indeksi is a standardized amount index (mean −0.13, standard deviation 0.98, right-skewed). There are no missing values, no duplicate rows, no alert without transactions, and no ID overlap between train and test. Alerts span 2025-01-01 to 2026-12-31 in both splits, so there is no time-based train/test split to account for.

Each alert has between 1 and 2,279 transactions (median 461, mean 499). The history window is at most 180 days and ends on the alert date.

Temporal leakage check: 0 of 10.0M transactions are dated after their alert. About 0.06% fall on the alert day itself, most of them stamped exactly 00:00:00. Because signal_sanasi has day granularity, we keep them as pre-alert history. The pipeline still filters to tx_time < alert_date + 1 day in case future rows are ever appended.

Section 4

Key observations from transaction history

↑ escalation

Cash share of volume

The strongest single signal (AUC 0.571). Accounts where cash makes up a larger share of the money moved escalate up to twice as often.

↓ escalation

Larger bank-transfer amounts

Higher mean outgoing bank-transfer size means less escalation (23% down to 11% across deciles; AUC 0.572 in that direction).

↓ escalation

Floor-clipped inflows

Incoming bank transfers clipped at the amount floor (−2.912) appear in 14% of alerts. Those alerts escalate at 9.8%, versus 18.4% for the rest.

↑ escalation

Smaller type-adjusted amounts

Once each transaction is compared with the norm for its type and direction, escalated accounts move smaller amounts across the board (AUC 0.543).

↑ escalation

More activity, international use

Transaction count (AUC 0.531), cash share (0.524) and international share (0.522) all lean toward escalation, but only slightly.

data artifact

Trigger burst at 23:57–23:59

About 40 transactions per alert are packed into the three minutes before the alert date. They're admissible (all before the alert) and equal across classes. We checked the leakage risk with an ablation.

no signal

Recency, time of day, weekday

No pre-alert acceleration, no night-time or weekend pattern. All of these score AUC ≈ 0.50–0.51, contrary to the classic AML intuition.

data quality

Clean relational structure

No transactions after the alert, no missing values, and no orphan or duplicated IDs. 15% of alerts have unsorted transactions, so we sort before computing any sequence feature.

Target distribution and behavioral pattern analysis

Because ROC-AUC depends only on ranking, we don't resample. The linear model uses balanced class weights, and the final blended score is Platt-calibrated so that the mean predicted probability matches the 17.2% base rate.

The picture that emerges: escalation is not driven by a dramatic event (bursts, night activity, a sudden change). It's driven by the composition of an account's flows: more of the money moving as cash, and smaller transfers and card payments than is normal for each channel. The signal is weak and spread across many features. No single feature scores above 0.58 AUC, and combining them lifts that to 0.644.

Model comparison (out-of-fold ROC-AUC, same 5 folds)

Validation and robustness checks

  • No early stopping on the scoring fold. Tree counts, Optuna hyperparameters (30 trials), the logistic C, and blend weights are all chosen on a different 5-fold split (seed 7). The tuning landscape was flat (0.634–0.642), so most of the gains come from features and blending, not tuning.
  • Permuted-label test: the same pipeline on shuffled labels scores 0.495. Adversarial validation (train versus test) scores 0.496, so the features are consistent across splits.
  • Trigger-burst ablation: pooling the burst gives 0.6435, a separate burst block 0.6425, burst-free timing features 0.6405, and excluding the burst entirely 0.6397. The model doesn't hinge on the artifact.
  • Also tried and rejected: a transaction-level model whose predictions are averaged per alert (0.556, because the signal lies in account-level composition, not in individual transactions), and a leaner amount-only feature set (tied).
Section 7

Conclusion: top findings

  1. What drives escalation is the composition of the money flow, not a single event. Accounts that move more of their volume in cash, and use smaller bank transfers and card payments than is typical for each channel, are the ones that get escalated.
  2. The data is temporally clean, but its timestamps aren't. No transaction comes after its alert, yet about 8% of transactions are packed into the three minutes before it. We traced this artifact, confirmed it's equal across classes, and showed the model is robust to removing it.
  3. Clipping carries signal. Amounts are clipped at a hard floor that only incoming bank transfers reach, and accounts with such inflows escalate at half the base rate.
  4. The classic AML intuitions don't hold here. There's no pre-alert acceleration, no night-time pattern, and no weekday pattern. Recency features are kept for completeness but carry almost no signal.
  5. The signal is weak and diffuse, so honest validation matters. The best single feature scores 0.57 AUC and the final blend 0.644 out of fold. That estimate uses no early stopping on the scoring folds, tunes on a separate split, and passes permuted-label and adversarial checks.
